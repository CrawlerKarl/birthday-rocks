The Pink Geode

A polished agate geode end, dyed hot pink, with a sparkling quartz cave in the middle

MineralAgate, with a quartz crystal cave
Size3.5 × 3.5 × 1.3 in (9 × 9 × 3.4 cm)about the size of a coffee mug across
Weight1.2 lb (544.9 g)about as heavy as a can of soup
Where it's fromCut and coloured in Brazil; the agate itself most likely from Rio Grande do Sul, southern Brazil

Agate is a form of quartz that grows ring by ring inside a gas bubble in old lava. Cut one open and you get this: concentric rings around a hollow, and the hollow lined with tiny quartz crystals that catch the light.

The hot pink is a dye, the classic finish for decorative Brazilian agate, and it is a big part of the look. The colour sits deepest in the sparkly cave and the rough outer skin, and under a UV lamp the pink bands glow a bright orange, which makes for a good party trick.

The sticker underneath says Made in Brazil, which is where it was sawn, polished and coloured. The agate itself almost certainly came out of the old lava country of Rio Grande do Sul in southern Brazil, where most of the world's agate is dug.

Three things to know

  1. Agate takes dye well because it is ever so slightly porous, and pale agate has been tinted for the decorative trade for a couple of centuries.
  2. Inside a geode the quartz crystals grow from the walls toward the middle, every point aimed at the hollow.
  3. The lava that this agate formed in flowed across southern Brazil and Uruguay around 135 million years ago.

The Honey Block

A block of honey-to-smoky calcite crystals that glows under a UV lamp

MineralCalcite
Size2.3 × 2.1 × 1.4 in (5.9 × 5.4 × 3.5 cm)about the size of a golf ball and a half
Weight5 oz (151.4 g)about as heavy as a baseball
Where it's fromNorthern Mexico, most likely, where most honey calcite like this is mined

Look at it from the side and you see how it grew: long, pointed calcite crystals packed side by side like a bundle of pencils, rising from a pale base and ending in a crust of small, sharp tips. The colour runs from honey to smoky brown, with darker streaks down some of the columns and a sprinkle of paler honey crystals perched on the very top.

Calcite is soft and friendly to test. A steel point leaves a mark on it, and a drop of vinegar makes it fizz, because the acid releases carbon dioxide from the stone. That is how we know exactly what it is.

It also has a party trick. Under a longwave UV lamp the whole block glows a soft creamy yellow, and under shortwave the pale lower crystals light up sky blue.

Three things to know

  1. Calcite can glow red, blue, yellow and more under ultraviolet light, and some pieces keep glowing for a moment after the lamp goes off.
  2. Collectors call pointed calcite crystals dogtooth spar, and the flat-topped kind nailhead spar.
  3. Calcite is the main mineral in limestone and marble, so it is in a good share of the buildings and statues you have ever seen.

The Peacock Cluster

A quartz crystal cluster finished with a titanium film that shimmers gold and violet

MineralQuartz, with a titanium shimmer coat
Size2.8 × 2.0 × 1.6 in (7 × 5.2 × 4 cm)about the size of a tennis ball
Weight6 oz (182 g)about as heavy as a smartphone
Where it's fromBrazilian geode country, most likely, with the finish added in a workshop

The crystals are stubby quartz, the kind that lines a geode, and the shimmer is a metal finish: the cluster goes into a vacuum chamber, metal vapour (usually titanium) settles onto every surface, and the film is so thin that it splits light into colours, the same trick as oil on a puddle. Gold on the flat side faces, violet and magenta on the tips, depending on the angle.

This finish goes by “titanium aura” or “flame aura.” Turn it over and the flat base is bare grey quartz, because the cluster lay face up in the chamber while the film settled.

Under a UV lamp it turns into a tiny mirror, which is exactly what a metal film should do.

Three things to know

  1. Quartz keeps time: a sliver of it vibrates at a perfectly steady rate when given a tiny electric nudge, which is the quartz in a quartz watch.
  2. The shimmer coat works like oil on water: a film so thin that light splits into colours as it bounces off.
  3. After feldspar, there is more quartz in the Earth’s crust than any other mineral.

The Green Marble

Polished malachite from the Congo's copper country

MineralMalachite
Size3.0 × 2.8 × 1.8 in (7.7 × 7.1 × 4.5 cm)about the size of a baseball
Weight16 oz (443.9 g)about as heavy as a football
Where it's fromKatanga Province, Democratic Republic of the Congo

Malachite is a copper mineral, and it grows where copper ore slowly weathers near the surface. It builds up in layers, which is why a polished piece shows rings, bull’s-eyes and rounded, knobbly sections: each ring is one more layer of green laid down over time.

This one was polished into a palm stone, and the pits and little open cavities came with it: they are where the layers grew around gaps, and the sparkle inside them is a lining of tiny crystals, the last ones to grow before the stone closed over. Polishers leave those alone, and they are the best part to catch in the light.

It came with a card naming Katanga Province in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. That is the Congolese side of the Copperbelt, and the mines around Kolwezi there supply much of the world’s malachite for polishing, so the card and the stone agree.

Three things to know

  1. Malachite is named after the mallow plant, whose leaves are the same green.
  2. Ground up, it was a green paint pigment from ancient Egypt until about 1800, and the Egyptians wore it as eye paint.
  3. The mines around Kolwezi in Katanga supply much of the world’s malachite for polishing, so this stone comes from the heart of malachite country.
