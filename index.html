The Pink Geode

A polished agate geode end, dyed hot pink, with a sparkling quartz cave in the middle

MineralAgate (banded chalcedony) with quartz
Size9 × 9 × 3.4 cmabout the size of a coffee mug across
Weight544.9 gabout as heavy as a can of soup
Where it's fromCut and coloured in Brazil; the agate itself most likely from Rio Grande do Sul, southern Brazil

Agate is banded silica that grows ring by ring inside a gas bubble in old lava. Cut one open and you get this: concentric rings around a hollow, and the hollow lined with tiny quartz crystals that catch the light.

The hot pink is a dye, the classic finish for decorative Brazilian agate, and it is a big part of the look. The colour sits deepest where the stone is porous, in the druse and the pits of the rind, and under a UV lamp the dyed bands glow a bright orange, which makes for a good party trick.

The sticker underneath says Made in Brazil, which is where it was sawn, polished and coloured. The agate itself almost certainly came out of the lava fields of Rio Grande do Sul in southern Brazil, the world's main source of this stuff.

Three things to know

  1. Pink agate this vivid owes its color to people: it is soaked in an aniline-type organic dye, which, unlike the older metal-salt colors, can fade in strong light.
  2. Agate is a banded form of chalcedony, a microcrystalline quartz porous enough to absorb dyes well, so pale or dull agate is often colored artificially.
  3. In a geode, the quartz crystals of the druzy lining grow around the cavity wall with each point aimed toward the hollow center.
Rock 2 of 4

The Honey Block

A block of honey-gold quartz crystals from the wall of a geode

MineralQuartz
Size5.9 × 5.4 × 3.5 cmabout the size of a golf ball and a half
Weight151.4 gabout as heavy as a baseball
Where it's fromNo label. Honey quartz of this kind usually comes from the amethyst geode country of southern Brazil or northern Uruguay

Look at it from the side and you see the structure: closely packed quartz columns rising from a milky white base and ending in a crust of small, sharp points. That is a slice of geode lining, the crystal wall that grew inward from the rock.

The honey colour has a story. Quartz this colour, growing from white roots, is usually amethyst that was gently heated: heat turns amethyst's purple to yellow and brown, and the result goes by the name citrine. It may also be natural smoky quartz. Either way it is the same mineral, and the colour runs right through the crystals.

The six-sided points are the giveaway for quartz. Calcite, the usual look-alike, makes dogtooth or rhomb-shaped tips, and these are not that.

Three things to know

  1. Amethyst loses its violet color when heated above about 200 to 300 °C (392 to 572 °F), and most citrine is made by heating amethyst or smoky quartz.
  2. Natural citrine tends to be pale; heated amethyst is usually a deeper yellow, orange or brown, and in geode pieces it is often darkest at the crystal tips and white near the base.
  3. Amethyst's purple comes from traces of iron plus natural irradiation in the quartz; heat-treated amethyst instead carries tiny amounts of the iron oxides hematite and goethite.
Rock 3 of 4

The Peacock Cluster

A quartz crystal cluster finished with a titanium film that shimmers gold and violet

MineralQuartz, with a titanium-type coating
Size7 × 5.2 × 4 cmabout the size of a tennis ball
Weight182 gabout as heavy as a smartphone
Where it's fromNo label. Clusters like this are usually Brazilian geode quartz, coated elsewhere

The crystals are stubby quartz, the kind that lines a geode, and the shimmer is a vapour-deposited film: the cluster goes into a vacuum chamber, metal vapour (usually titanium) settles onto every surface, and the film is so thin that light interferes with itself in it, the same trick as oil on a puddle. Gold on the flat prism faces, violet and magenta on the tips, depending on the angle.

This finish goes by “titanium aura” or “flame aura.” Turn it over and the flat base is bare grey quartz, because the cluster lay face up in the chamber while the film settled.

Under a UV lamp it does nothing but reflect, which is exactly what a metal mirror should do.

Three things to know

  1. The metallic sheen is not the mineral: quartz, like other crystals, can be coated with metal vapors for an attractive shine, a finish the decorative market calls titanium or flame aura.
  2. Quartz is the mineral that defines 7 on the Mohs hardness scale, so a steel blade will not scratch the crystals under this film.
  3. Quartz is piezoelectric: it develops an electric charge under mechanical stress, which is why quartz crystal oscillators keep time in watches and clocks.
Rock 4 of 4

The Green Marble

Polished malachite from the Congo's copper country

MineralMalachite
Size7.7 × 7.1 × 4.5 cmabout the size of a baseball
Weight443.9 gabout as heavy as a football
Where it's fromKatanga Province, Democratic Republic of the Congo (from its card)

Malachite is a copper mineral, a copper carbonate, and it grows where copper ore slowly weathers near the surface. It builds up in layers, which is why a polished piece shows rings, bull’s-eyes and rounded, knobbly sections: each ring is one more layer of green laid down over time.

This one was polished into a palm stone, and the pits and little open cavities came with it: they are where the layers grew around gaps, and one of them still has its crystal lining. A tiny pit near a cluster of knobs holds a dab of clear resin, a finishing touch the polishers use.

The card that came with it says Katanga Province, Democratic Republic of the Congo. That is the Congolese side of the Copperbelt, and the mines around Kolwezi there supply much of the world’s malachite for polishing, so the card and the stone agree.

Three things to know

  1. Malachite takes its name from the Greek for the mallow plant, whose leaves match its green.
  2. Mines around Kolwezi in Katanga supply much of the world's malachite for polishing, the material that becomes palm stones, boxes and tabletops.
  3. Malachite is a copper carbonate hydroxide that forms where copper sulfide ores weather near the surface; its concentric bands and rounded crusts record that slow growth.

How this was made

Each rock was photographed on a small turntable, 48 frames per turn, which is what the spins are. The same photos went through Apple's Object Capture to build the 3D models. The UV lamps are the standard 255, 310 and 365 nanometre set collectors use; the dyed agate was the only one to glow.

The facts come from Wikipedia and a couple of geology papers, checked against the source. Every identification is from photographs and the usual tests, which is good but not a lab. If you ever want one of these back for a closer look, just ask.
